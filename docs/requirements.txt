01 / Purpose, authority and reading guide
This document consolidates the dream-version game discussed with Joey Hersh on November 16, 2026. It is a
requirements and visual-reference document, not a production schedule, a new brainstorming round, or a mandate
to reproduce the old game-jam build. “Moxie” identifies the protagonist and serves as this document’s project
label; a final game title has not been selected.
The game is an isometric, music-linked puzzle-combat adventure in which a human scavenger records multiple
versions of herself onto cassette tracks. Those performances combine into one evolving encounter. The central
experience is discovering a clever plan, executing its pieces, and watching them become a coordinated, cinematic
performance.
The source hierarchy
Latest explicit user decisions control. When the conversation revises an earlier answer, the later answer
supersedes it. Repeated assistant summaries do not overrule the user. An assistant suggestion is not automatically
a requirement merely because the discussion moved on.
The selected images are visual references, not hidden gameplay specifications. They establish appearance,
materials, lighting and presentation. Text generated inside an image, incidental props, a pose, or an unapproved
measurement does not silently create lore, abilities or production requirements.
The original documents provide historical and technical context. The game-jam archive identifies itself as a 2025
record compiled on November 16, 2026. The supplied Harmonograph page describes an existing repository and its
documented state. Both are reproduced in full in the appendices, but neither supersedes the current design
conversation.
Editorial implications are labeled. This document sometimes expresses a confirmed rule as an equation, a state
table or a verification scenario. Those are interpretations for implementation, not additional decisions made by the
user. Where two desired behaviors need reconciliation, the conflict is retained explicitly instead of silently “fixed.”

Requirement identifiers such as TIME-01 and GHOST-03 support implementation discussions and testing. They
are editorial organization. They do not imply that a formal prioritization or engineering sign-off has already
occurred.

What is included
The main chapters cover the concept, clock model, recording workflow, repeated and reversed playback, death,
hypothetical post-death recording, desynchronization, combat, enemy behavior, bosses, level structure,
equipment, music, interface, cinematic replay, Moxie, the dungeon and the art direction. A later section retains the
original ideation branches and implementation history. The complete supplied text documents remain available as
printed appendices and as original text-file attachments inside this PDF.
Four selected visual assets are reproduced without redesign: the second single-character Moxie concept with
futuristic belt and wrist equipment; the corrected anime pose/reference sheet; the warmer isometric upper-level
board; and the darker, rune-inscribed metal lower-level board. Additional detail crops, where used, are crops of
those same assets rather than new art.
Corrections that matter most
An actively recorded ghost can interact. The later correction is decisive: while recording after death, the player
still follows normal physical movement constraints and can attack or operate switches in that provisional run. A
dead prerecorded self cannot do those things during later playback.
Editing an older committed cassette resets every cassette. It does not merely erase the recordings after that
cassette. Only the currently recorded, not-yet-advanced cassette can be freely retried while retaining earlier
committed tapes.
Enemy reactivity does not require randomness. Enemies can obey consistent rules and still behave differently
when another cassette changes their available targets or the battlefield. “Recorded selves are deterministic,
enemies are not” was an imprecise earlier assistant description.
The current game is isometric, equipment-driven and not a roguelike. Side-view precision platforming, a
branching level map, skill-point spending and point-based respecs were explored and then replaced. The final
progression direction is a fixed campaign path with revisiting and between-level equipment changes.
The music must remain coherent. Earlier suggestions about voices drifting away from the shared harmony are not
a mandate to create discordant music. Death and desynchronization should affect generative parameters without
abandoning the user’s clean, elegant musical target.

02 / The game at a glance
Core concept
Moxie is a scrappy, expressive, tomboyish human tech scavenger. She enters ancient ruins by choice, seeking loot
rather than trying to escape captivity. The ruins have been retrofitted with technology. Their deeper layers reveal
more rune-inscribed metal, neon machinery and temporal systems. The ultimate treasure at the bottom is a literal
time machine.
A belt-clipped Walkman is her signature time-recording device. A level consists of one complete
cassette-recording attempt, normally using up to four tracks. She records one performance, returns to the same
starting beat, and records the next while earlier performances play alongside her. Different tape speeds change
how each performance occupies the shared world timeline and how forcefully it interacts with that world.
The player is not commanding four adaptive companions. They are creating four precise performances. Earlier
selves do not improvise new attacks or steer toward new targets. Enemies, missiles, doors and other world objects
can respond to the changing encounter, so the outcome of an old performance is not guaranteed to repeat.
The music is generated using concepts and capabilities from Harmonograph, Joey’s existing GitHub repository,
j o e y 1 2 7 2 5 / h a r m o n o g r a p h . Each cassette is also a musical layer with its own generative function. The layers share
a coherent harmonic and timing framework. Player actions and world sounds become rhythmically integrated
rather than simply sitting on top of an unrelated soundtrack.
The intended feeling
PILLAR-01 — Feel clever. The primary emotional goal is “I found a good solution to a hard problem.” Success
should feel earned by understanding the system, choosing the right resource at the right time, and constructing a
favorable future game state.
PILLAR-02 — Plan through iterations. The player thinks across recordings. Moment-to-moment execution matters,
but the larger challenge is selecting where each version goes, which tape speed it uses, and the order in which the
player records the solution.
PILLAR-03 — Combat is part of the puzzle. This is not a pure puzzle game with combat attached, and not an
ordinary action game with occasional switches. Enemy targeting, momentum, knockback, survival and spatial
timing are puzzle materials.
PILLAR-04 — Fixed performances, changing consequences. The player can rely on an earlier self’s choreography
while manipulating the environment that choreography encounters. Rescue, distraction, redirection, missed
attacks and deliberate desynchronization all arise from that relationship.
PILLAR-05 — A coherent musical result. The soundtrack should remain as elegant and clean as possible
throughout play. It should not invalidate a mechanically correct solution for being rhythmically unusual.
PILLAR-06 — The solution becomes a performance. A successful attempt culminates in an automatic,
non-interactive, cinematic replay at the world’s true beat speed. The replay adds expressive musical visuals while
preserving what actually happened.

Boundaries of the vision
There are four cassette identities, not four unrelated playable characters. Moxie remains the central character.
Occasional supporting characters, including possible tutorial encounters, can reveal her personality and establish
that other people exist in the world. The user’s Celeste comparison concerns this light-touch supporting cast, not a
demand for a similarly structured story or platforming system.
The game should have personality without requiring an elaborate narrative. The human scavenger, loot-driven
descent, ancient musical technology and final time machine are enough to anchor the mechanical fantasy. The
exact civilization, chronology, dialogue, cast and ending remain unspecified.
A time-loop shooter described as chess-like was mentioned as an inspiration, but its title was uncertain in the
conversation. This document preserves the intended comparison—planning future states with current
resources—without assigning the reference to an unverified game.
The original inspiration list also included Heat, Balatro, Slay the Spire, D&D, XCOM, fighting games and other
ideas. These belong in the historical context, not a requirement that the final game include racing, cards, tactical
turns or roguelike runs.

03 / Time, tape speed and the shared world
Three different meanings of time
TIME-01 — One shared world. Platforms, enemies, doors and projectiles inhabit one physical encounter. They do
not exist in four independent worlds. The master musical timeline is the common reference for their interactions.
TIME-02 — A consistent experience while recording. For a given level, recording one cassette takes the same
amount of actual playtime regardless of the chosen tape speed. The character’s basic movement and
responsiveness should feel consistent to the person holding the controls. The world’s apparent speed changes
relative to that active character.
TIME-03 — Different durations on the master timeline. A faster tape occupies less master-world time for one
traversal of its recording; a slower tape occupies more. Recordings are not stretched to make their ends coincide.
The user explicitly rejected that because it would change where moving platforms and other objects are when
actions occur.
Distinguish wall-clock playtime, local cassette progress and master-world beats. The conversation sometimes
called both wall-clock time and true-speed world playback “real time”; implementation and interface terminology
must be explicit.
Musical duration, not a universal number of seconds
TIME-04 — Author duration in measures. A level’s recording allowance is defined musically. The number of bars
can vary by level to suit the puzzle. The repeated example of a 15-second recording was illustrative, not a locked
duration for every level.
TIME-05 — Tune length by playtesting. No universal minimum, maximum or target range was finalized. The user
expressly deferred that choice. The later question asking for a fixed ten-second duration was a retread, not a new
decision.
TIME-06 — Half/normal/double relationships. There should be a middle speed with a faster and slower option,
related by doubling or halving. Calling these 0.5×, 1× and 2× is a convenient normalization. Calling the same
relative choices quarter/half/base changes the labels, not the intended relationship.
The original jam list also included 4×. Quadruple speed remains historical or optional; the later conversation clearly
established powers-of-two relationships but did not independently finalize a fourth selectable speed. Four
cassettes must not be confused with four speed settings.
A consistent mathematical interpretation
This editorial derivation expresses the later recording/playback rules, not new tuning.
Let T be the level’s fixed wall-clock recording allowance, u be elapsed local recording time, and s be the selected
playback multiplier relative to the middle option. A simple mapping is:
m a s t e r _ t i m e = l o c a l _ t i m e / t a p e _ s p e e d
o n e _ t r a v e r s a l _ d u r a t i o n = T / t a p e _ s p e e d
While recording the active cassette, the world advances at 1/s of its normal master-time rate relative to the
player’s real seconds. An earlier tape j is displayed at s_j/s_active relative to the active player’s local time. This
preserves the same world events and spatial relationships rather than assigning every entity an unrelated clock.

Expressed in musical coordinates, a local tape of B bars occupies B/s master bars under this interpretation. The

Important unresolved timing tension
Early in the conversation, the desired tradeoff was described as “faster hits harder, gets less far, and is harder to
control,” with slower versions easier to control. Later, the user explicitly required equal real-time recording lengths
and consistent character movement while the world changes speed. The inverse-clock mapping above implies that
a faster cassette sees a slower world during recording, not a faster one. Equal local duration and equal local
free-space movement also do not automatically produce a shorter maximum walking distance.
These are not grounds to discard the design. They are open balancing and terminology issues. Harder fast-tape
play might emerge from timing, compressed world coverage or other encounter constraints, but no mechanism
accomplishing that was approved. The requirement is to preserve the later timing rules and test the earlier
intended tradeoff, not silently reverse a clock or invent a movement penalty.
Likewise, the user requested a tape-speed-sensitive “five, six, seven, eight” count-in. Its exact relation to local tape
tempo versus master tempo needs to be specified consistently with the recording model. Simply accelerating
every sound when s increases would not automatically match the inverse visual time scale.
The whole-attempt horizon
TIME-07 — A common overall horizon. Faster tracks may stop, repeat or reverse while the longest relevant track
continues. The user described matching the slowest option. With 0.5×, 1× and 2× tapes and T = 15 seconds, a
30-second master horizon accommodates one, two and four forward traversals respectively.
OPEN — What determines that horizon? The discussion alternated between the slowest selected cassette and the
slowest available option. A level-authored fixed horizon, a slowest-selected horizon and a slowest-allowed horizon
are not identical when the player selects only fast tapes. The final choice was not made.
The winning replay must cover the actual intended master-timeline encounter, without arbitrary padding or
stretching. The exact duration rule must also cover introductory levels with fewer than four tapes.

04 / Recording, commitment, retries and
evaluation
One level means one cassette attempt
REC-01 — A level is one complete recording sequence. It can contain multiple rooms or smaller challenges, but
those challenges share the same cassette attempt. They are not independent encounters with their own automatic
cassette resets.
REC-02 — Every recording starts at beat zero. Cassette two does not continue the world from the end of cassette
one. The same encounter is reconstructed from its initial physical state with the existing tapes playing and one
additional player-controlled performance being recorded.
REC-03 — Earlier tapes are visible during recording. The player sees all previously recorded selves alongside the
current self. They do not see future cassettes that have not yet been recorded. This is sequential planning with
visible prior commitments, not an omniscient editor of an already complete solution.
REC-04 — The current cassette can be retried before advancing. A player can discard and rerecord the current
take while keeping earlier committed cassettes. Choosing to advance commits that take into the sequence.
REC-05 — Returning to an earlier cassette resets all tapes. Once the player has moved to a later cassette,
changing an earlier one requires starting the entire level attempt again from cassette one. The user explicitly
corrected “wipe the later ones” to “wipes all of them.”
A normal recording cycle
The player enters the level with the equipment selected between levels. Before recording a tape, they choose its
speed and, once unlocked, its end-of-tape behavior. A “five, six, seven, eight” count-in prepares the performance.
The count-in should be visual as well as audible and should communicate the selected timing relationship.
Recording starts at master beat zero. Earlier tapes replay against the shared world; the current cassette receives
live control. A continuous timeline with beat markers is visible. The player records movement, attacks, dodges and
interactions for the level’s allowed local duration. Death does not stop this recording; the special post-death
recording rules apply.
Before moving on, the player can retry the current cassette. On advancing, the next recording again starts from
beat zero with the earlier takes retained. There is no newly created future actor before that actor has been
recorded.
After the final recording, playback may begin automatically. The user approved automatic playback, but this does
not erase the requirement to offer a retry/commit decision at the appropriate point. The exact interaction
between an automatic preview and committing a take is an interface detail, not permission to auto-commit
irrevocably.
Which outcome counts
REC-06 — Evaluate the final combined encounter. The user described the fourth track’s encounter as the one that
determines whether the level passes. A kill achieved in cassette one’s original recording is not banked if that kill
does not happen in the final combined simulation.

REC-07 — Objective plus survivor. Completion requires the level objective and at least one surviving cassette
character. It does not require all four to survive. Sacrificing one or more tapes can be a valid plan.
For introductory levels using fewer than four recordings, the natural extension is to evaluate the final required
recording. This is a working interpretation of the later one-cassette tutorial allowance; the exact early-completion
rules were not finalized.
REC-08 — Failure does not force an immediate cut. Let the full playback finish by default so the player can
observe the failure. Provide a way to end the attempt or reset early rather than trapping the player in a known
failed sequence.
What resets and what persists
Physical encounter state—enemy positions, damage, gates and interactions—must be recalculated for the new
combined run. A previously killed enemy can survive in the new run because the circumstances changed. This is
different from reviving an enemy within one uninterrupted run.
The recordings themselves persist when retrying the current cassette or advancing. Equipment ownership and
campaign progress belong to the campaign layer, not a bank of provisional combat outcomes. Special bosses that
remember across recordings may be an intentional exception to ordinary state reset; their memory policy is still
open.
OPEN — Recording pauses. The dialogue around pausing was interrupted and the next question began before the
answer was fully disambiguated. The overall intent is a real-time performed take with planning between iterations.
A dedicated mid-take tactical-pause feature was not conclusively approved or rejected.
OPEN — Preview, skip and reset details. The player can reset early on failure. Scrubbing, beat stepping,
independent tape soloing, timeline editing and replay export were raised as possibilities in assistant lists but were
not selected as requirements.

05 / What a tape preserves, and what it does not
Fixed choreography
PLAY-01 — Preserve the performance. Earlier selves repeat the same movement, positioning, attack direction and
action timing. They do not acquire adaptive aim or choose new behaviors during playback. The active player is
creating choreography, not training an autonomous companion.
PLAY-02 — Preserve attempts, not old results. A recorded melee swing is a swing at a location and time. Whether
it hits an enemy is determined by the current run. A switch interaction is attempted in the current world and
subject to the actor’s current state. It does not force an old door state into existence regardless of what is present.
PLAY-03 — Recompute world responses. Enemies can move, retarget, be displaced and die differently. Doors can
be closed at a different time. A launched homing missile can choose a different nearest enemy. These changed
consequences are fundamental to the puzzle.
PLAY-04 — Do not casually displace a recorded player. Enemy stagger or knockback should not alter the
prerecorded player’s path. The user specifically found those responses unsuitable for earlier selves while still
allowing them to die. Unexpected environmental obstruction is handled by desynchronization rather than
rewriting the trajectory.
Why input-only replay is not enough by itself
The old build used a circular buffer of inputs/actions. That is historical implementation context, not proof that
input replay alone satisfies the current requirement. Replaying identical inputs against changed collision geometry
can produce different positions, which contradicts fixed choreography.
A current implementation therefore needs to preserve the intended path and events, or otherwise guarantee the
equivalent result. A trajectory-plus-event representation is one possible approach. The precise storage model,
sample rate, interpolation and engine integration were not chosen in this conversation.
A useful conceptual split is: the tape owns the intended performance; the world owns collision and interaction
outcomes; the actor’s alive/ghost/desynchronized state determines whether its attempted actions are physically
effective. This split explains the design without prescribing a software architecture.
End-of-tape behavior
PLAY-05 — Three behaviors. The player should eventually be able to choose, per cassette, whether it stops at the
end, restarts from the beginning, or plays backward. These controls are introduced later rather than exposing all
complexity immediately.
Stop: the tape ceases its recorded action sequence at its endpoint. Whether the body remains solid, holds a plate,
is targetable, disappears, or counts as the required survivor is not yet specified.
Repeat: the recorded self cuts straight back to its starting position and performs the same recording again. It does
not walk back or stretch the original take. The larger encounter continues. Two, four or more traversals can fit
depending on relative speeds and the common horizon.
Reverse: the tape reaches its endpoint and retraces the recorded action sequence backward. Alternating forward
and backward playback—often called ping-pong in the discussion—was the working description. The exact
behavior after repeated endpoints still needs an explicit event rule.

06 / Death, ghosts and hypothetical survival
The essential distinction
GHOST-01 — Death never truncates the take being recorded. When the current character dies, the player
continues controlling and recording the rest of that cassette. There must be a major visual distinction showing that
this is a ghost or hypothetical continuation.
GHOST-02 — The recording ghost remains physically constrained. While actively recording after death, the
character cannot simply walk through a wall or closed door. The user revised the earlier freely phasing
recording-ghost idea specifically because it could record impossible routes for a subsequently rescued character.
GHOST-03 — The recording ghost can affect that provisional run. Its attacks can kill enemies and its switch
operations can open doors while the user finishes the take. Those effects help the player perform a possible
continuation. They do not automatically carry into later recordings.
GHOST-04 — The recording ghost takes no further damage. The character is already dead in that provisional
performance. Continuing to take lethal hits would not usefully represent the hypothetical route being recorded.
GHOST-05 — Playback ghosts are non-interactive. When an earlier cassette dies during a later run, it continues
along the recorded trajectory but cannot damage enemies, operate switches or physically influence the world. It
does not take further damage and can pass through obstacles to preserve its path.
GHOST-06 — Survival is recalculated each run. “Was recorded as a ghost” is not a permanent property of the
action. A later cassette can prevent an earlier death, making the post-death recorded actions effective. Conversely,
a previously successful recording can die earlier in the new encounter and lose the effects of its remaining actions.

Example: rescue a future switch activation
During cassette one’s original take, the character dies at local second six. The player continues as a visibly distinct
recording ghost, kills an enemy at second ten, activates a switch at second twelve, and finishes at second fifteen.
Those actions affect that provisional run so the route can be performed.
During cassette two, cassette one begins from beat zero. If it dies again at six, its later attack does not kill the
enemy and its switch does not open the door. Its ghost continues along the recorded route even if that door is
now closed.
If cassette two prevents the fatal hit, cassette one is alive at ten and twelve. Its attack and switch operation can
now be physically effective, assuming the world still presents valid targets and the actor remains synchronized.
The player is not merely recovering health; they are enabling a planned future contribution.
Example: a formerly safe take becomes fatal
Cassette one originally survives all fifteen seconds. In the next recording, enemy targeting changes because
another self is present. Cassette one walks into a newly aimed projectile at second twelve and dies. The final three
seconds play as a non-interactive ghost even though those frames were originally recorded alive.
This was an explicit user example. It establishes that the cassette’s original life/death annotation cannot be
replayed as an immutable script. The current simulation determines physical effectiveness.
Provisional effects must not become permanent history
The actively recorded ghost’s world changes are a rehearsal aid, not authoritative later-run results. A later run
must resolve the events again using the playback character’s actual alive/desynchronized status. Copying an
enemy’s old death, or leaving a door open because the recording ghost opened it previously, would violate the
mechanic.
This also means rescuing a character does not guarantee every later interaction succeeds. Other tapes can have
changed target positions, switches or access. A rescued but obstructed character can still desynchronize. The
system preserves what the actor attempts, not a promise that its hypothetical route will be fully effective.
The final-tape edge case
OPEN — The fourth active recording can itself die and continue hypothetically. The user wants the fourth
combined encounter to determine victory, but also wants post-death active recording actions to affect that
recording run only. Therefore a final success determination cannot mistakenly count objectives completed solely
by the fourth recording ghost’s provisional actions.
A non-interactive validation of all tapes under playback rules is one possible resolution; an
authoritative-versus-provisional event distinction during the last take is another. These are implementation
proposals, not an additional gameplay mode already approved. The required outcome is clear: a dead playback self
cannot win the level through interactions it should no longer have.

07 / Desynchronization and the recovery spark
Why desynchronization exists
A fixed recorded path can become impossible in a later run. For example, cassette one passed through an open
door, but cassette two now closes that door before cassette one reaches it. Letting the old character run in place
would invalidate the positions and timing of its later attacks. Simply phasing through the door and becoming solid
on the other side would make the closed door largely irrelevant.
The selected direction preserves the visible choreography while giving actual traversability a consequence: the
recorded projection continues, but a second, physically constrained recovery position must catch up before the
actor becomes interactive again.
The two positions
DESYNC-01 — The recording remains the target. The desynchronized projection continues following its exact
intended performance. It does not detour, wait at the wall, choose a different attack or move the entire tape later
in time.
DESYNC-02 — A recovery spark represents the reachable position. A simple, relatively naive navigation agent
attempts to reach the prerecorded character. It moves fairly quickly but not instantaneously and follows the kinds
of traversable routes the player is allowed to use.
DESYNC-03 — Geometry can block the spark. A closed wall or inaccessible route can prevent recovery. The spark
cannot solve a locked-door puzzle by operating a switch for itself. It is a catch-up mechanism, not a second
autonomous puzzle-solving character.
DESYNC-04 — A valid detour costs time. An open, out-of-the-way route may eventually allow the spark to catch
the projection. A direct route that was blocked can be reopened by another cassette, enabling faster recovery.
Merely finding empty space on the far side of the wall is not sufficient.
DESYNC-05 — Resynchronization occurs on catch-up. When the reachable recovery position rejoins the recorded
position, the character becomes physically effective again. The exact distance tolerance, collision clearance,
contact behavior and whether a beat boundary is involved are tuning decisions still open.
Visibility and feel
DESYNC-06 — Clearly visible, small and ephemeral. The user wants the spark easy to track, but not a large second
character. A small glowing point or similarly ephemeral mark is the established direction. Invisible recovery logic
was considered and not preferred.
The distinction between the actor’s projection and the spark must be understandable in an isometric room. A
visually satisfying merge is a working art suggestion, not a requirement for a particular particle effect. The cassette
channel should also communicate this state through its musical behavior.
Interaction during desynchronization
DESYNC-07 — Temporarily harmless and invulnerable. The user positively considered engineered invulnerability
and continued developing puzzles around it. The working rule is that a desynchronized character can neither deal
nor receive damage and cannot operate world mechanisms while desynchronized.

08 / Movement, momentum and combat
Perspective and movement priorities
COMBAT-01 — Isometric play. The user explicitly selected isometric after recognizing that jumping and vertical
space were not central to the imagined puzzles. Navigation through rooms, spatial timing, mechanisms, positioning
and combat matter more than precision jumping.
Relatively flat 2.5D geometry was the assistant’s working framing. The user selected the perspective, not a
particular projection angle, engine renderer or ban on all height changes. Stairs, ramps, visual elevation and
interconnected spaces can exist without making precision platforming the defining skill.
Movement should feel responsive and consistent while the player is recording any tape. The world’s relative pace
and the tape’s forceful effect in master time provide the variation. General control bindings were discussed but not
fully selected: WASD plus mouse-facing and controller twin-stick input were suggestions; movement-facing
attacks were another option. Melee does not require one particular aiming scheme.
The combat vocabulary
COMBAT-02 — Melee is primary. Earlier selves always strike at the recorded angle or place. The game should
derive depth from positioning and coordinating these strikes, rather than allowing old characters to automatically
chase or aim at new targets.
COMBAT-03 — Light and heavy attacks. Both should exist. Tape speed changes their timing relative to the shared
encounter. A fast-tape light attack can look like a blurred flurry; a slow-tape heavy attack can be deeply impractical
because its commitment occupies so much world time.
COMBAT-04 — Dodge with invincibility frames. A dodge is required, and the user accepted i-frames. Its behavior
should feel meaningfully different across tape speeds. Keeping its animation and invulnerability interval consistent
in local tape time, then mapping them to master time, is the working interpretation. Exact distance, cooldown,
startup and invulnerability fractions remain unchosen.
A simpler action set is desirable because coordinating timelines supplies the larger complexity. That does not
prohibit expressive animation, directional attacks or equipment abilities; it does mean an elaborate fighting-game
input system has not been requested.
Momentum is the central combat variable
MOM-01 — Movement and tape speed feed momentum. The user clarified the causal chain repeatedly: actual
movement/current speed and tape speed affect effective momentum; momentum affects damage, stagger and
knockback. These should not feel like unrelated stat bonuses attached to the tape selector.
MOM-02 — Momentum affects more than damage. High-force attacks must be able to displace enemies more
strongly and influence stagger. Knockback is a primary puzzle interaction, not a decorative animation.
MOM-03 — Direction matters. The user discussed actual movement as part of momentum. Treating momentum
as direction-sensitive is a working physical interpretation: running into a strike, moving away, and changing
direction need not transfer identical force. The precise calculation has not been selected.
MOM-04 — Attack type transfers force differently. Light and heavy attacks need distinct feel. Their exact base
damage, reach, mass-like contribution, force transfer, startup and recovery were not finalized. A heavy attack
favoring larger impulse and a light attack favoring repeated application were suggestions, not settled formulas.

The Ant-Man analogy was used to describe concentrating an action through time rather than through mass or size.
It is a design metaphor for temporal impact, not a requirement for physically accurate relativistic mechanics or for

A useful implementation interpretation, not a fixed formula
For the time mapping in Chapter 03, a local velocity v_local corresponds to v_master = s × v_local. A combat model
can therefore derive effective momentum from motion in master-world time, with attack motion and equipment
supplying additional terms as appropriate.
This interpretation matters because tape speed should not be accidentally counted twice: once by converting
velocity into master time and again by applying an unexplained identical multiplier. There may still be deliberate
balancing curves, caps or nonlinear effects, but their values and definitions were not chosen here.
The conversation did not settle whether a stationary character’s weapon swing alone generates all required
momentum, whether movement inertia is persistent, or whether damage and knockback share the same curve.
The requirement is a legible common cause, not a premature physics equation.
Dynamic enemy displacement
MOM-05 — Knockback changes the encounter. Enemies retain their newly simulated positions within the current
run and react from there. A cassette can knock an enemy into or out of a turret’s line of fire, an earlier melee
swing, another cassette’s attack or an environmental hazard.
A previously successful swing may miss after another tape changes the enemy’s path. That is expected. The player
should also be able to arrange the converse: a previously ineffective attack becomes useful because another self
delivers the enemy into it.
The user accepted real displacement, not a fixed hit animation that snaps enemies back to predetermined
positions. However, enemy mass, resistance, collision with other enemies, shield behavior and boss immunity rules
are still content-design choices.
MOM-06 — Do not apply enemy knockback rules to fixed player recordings. Enemy stagger and displacement are
desired. Enemy attacks staggering or knocking a recorded player off its path were specifically not desired. These
two uses of “stagger” must remain separate in implementation and documentation.
Health and lethality
HEALTH-01 — Each player cassette is one-hit lethal. A qualifying hit kills that individual tape character. It does not
instantly end the whole attempt or truncate the active recording. Dodging or desynchronization can prevent a hit
from qualifying under their respective immunity rules.
HEALTH-02 — Most ordinary enemies die in one hit. Some enemies should take two or three hits. These are
intended survivability bands, not immutable counts independent of momentum and equipment. The user
specifically mentioned an upgrade allowing a two-hit enemy to die in one hit.
HEALTH-03 — Bosses have health bars. Boss fights allow sustained damage and multi-cassette coordination
instead of using only the ordinary enemy hit-count model. Exact health totals and display treatment are not
selected.
HEALTH-04 — One surviving self is enough. The objective must be achieved and at least one cassette must
survive. Which cassette survives is not prescribed. Three sacrifices can be valid if the remaining character satisfies
the completion condition.

09 / Homing missiles and autonomous objects
A limited exception to fixed targeting
MISSILE-01 — Secondary ranged attack. Melee remains primary, but the user proposed a guided missile as an
interesting secondary attack. It provides a way for a deterministic recorded action to create an object whose later
behavior is dynamic.
MISSILE-02 — Fixed launch event. The cassette records when and where it fires. The character does not rewrite
that firing decision in response to the new world. The exact relationship between a recorded facing angle and the
missile’s initial orientation is not yet specified.
MISSILE-03 — Acquire the nearest enemy once at launch. The user explicitly chose one-time target acquisition.
The missile then tracks that target rather than continually switching to whichever enemy has become nearest.
MISSILE-04 — Dynamic flight. The missile is a world object, not a prerecorded person. It can pursue the currently
selected target’s simulated motion without violating the fixed-player-performance rule.
Why it matters for puzzles
Suppose cassette one fires at a particular moment. During the original recording, enemy A is nearest. In a later run,
cassette two knocks enemy B closer before that launch event. The same prerecorded firing action can now
produce a missile aimed at B. The changed target comes from the object’s launch-time logic, not from cassette one
deciding to aim differently.
Once the missile has chosen B, moving A closer does not change its target. This makes targeting manipulation a
discrete timing problem rather than an indefinitely shifting nearest-target contest.
A playback missile launches only when the actor is physically able to perform that action: alive and synchronized.
The actively controlled recording ghost is the known provisional exception; it may generate effects in that take
which must not be copied into later authoritative playback without survival.
Not yet specified
The assistant described missiles as “limited,” but the user did not choose ammunition, charges, a cooldown or an
explicit scarcity rule. These remain open. Do not make ammunition management a mandatory puzzle dimension
based solely on that wording.
There is no final rule for firing with no enemies present, equal-distance target ties, line-of-sight requirements,
walls, turn speed, projectile lifetime, target death before impact, or whether a missile persists after its owner dies
or desynchronizes.
The missile’s behavior during reversed tape playback also remains open. The earlier returning-bullet example does
not automatically define the behavior of a later homing projectile. It may need an explicit backward event type, but
no solution was selected.
Changing weapons was initially uncertain. The later equipment-based progression explicitly allows found weapons
and armor to modify or add abilities. The exact weapon roster, including whether every loadout retains the missile,
is still undefined.

10 / Enemies, targeting and bosses outside the

Understandable responses, not fixed enemy films
AI-01 — Enemies react to the current encounter. They may aim at the nearest player, move toward the farthest,
close a gap or follow other type-specific logic. The user used these as examples of rules, not a finalized list of
enemy classes.
AI-02 — More selves change circumstances, not necessarily the rule. An enemy can behave differently with two,
three or four selves because its nearest target, threats or accessible paths changed. It does not need a special
“two-player behavior mode” solely because a second tape exists.
AI-03 — Preserve player choreography despite enemy changes. Enemies can dodge a previous attack in the sense
of being somewhere else due to their simulation, while the recorded player still swings at the same place. Later
cassettes can herd, lure or knock enemies into those fixed attacks.
AI-04 — Legibility matters. The desired feeling is strategic, not arbitrary. Consistent targeting rules make a
changed outcome explainable. Enemy behavior can be reactive and still deterministic given the same initial
conditions, inputs and internal state. No requirement for random action selection was established.
The assistant suggested drawing aggro, staggering and forced movement as tools for controlling enemy behavior.
Momentum-driven stagger and knockback were later confirmed; a separate taunt or explicit aggro ability was not.
Encounter scales
AI-05 — Coordination at the encounter level. Most fights should require coordination, but not every individual
enemy needs multiple actors. One self may handle one or two weaker enemies while another works elsewhere.
Tough enemies or bosses may demand two, three or four selves acting together.
This allows allocation puzzles and focus-fire puzzles within the same system. The player might divide four
recordings across threats, or concentrate them on one enemy that has a narrow opening. Requiring all four tapes
to strike every ordinary enemy would misrepresent the user’s clarification.
AI-06 — Enemy attacks can be projectile-based. The player’s melee focus does not restrict enemy weapons.
Turrets and aimed projectile attacks are explicitly part of the design discussion.
AI-07 — Attacks follow musical timing. Enemies should fire on beats or subdivisions. Exact per-enemy grids and

directly modify the player cassettes’ generative wave functions.
Whether dead ghosts or desynchronized projections remain targetable by enemy decision logic was not settled.
Invulnerability alone does not answer whether an enemy should continue shooting at them; that policy can
substantially affect puzzles.
Special bosses
BOSS-01 — At least one boss can exist outside the loop. The user wants at least one, possibly multiple, bosses
that respond to prior recordings and can adapt across iterations. This intention was reaffirmed in the current
conversation; it is not merely an abandoned jam-era idea.

11 / Level structure, pressure and puzzle
vocabulary
Level definition and challenge allocation
LEVEL-01 — One level, one tape attempt. A level is the shared recording sequence for its cassette tracks. Multiple
rooms or subproblems can coexist within it. There is no reset simply because the player crosses into another room.
LEVEL-02 — Typical levels need two to four cassettes. Early teaching levels can use one. A single-cassette lesson
might demonstrate that sufficiently high momentum breaks a particular kind of wall.
LEVEL-03 — Combine small problems within the four-self budget. The user’s examples were one one-self problem
plus one three-self problem; two two-self problems; or one four-self problem. These examples describe how
demands can be distributed inside one level, not a mandate for a sequence of separately reset puzzle rooms.
A cassette can potentially contribute to more than one task over time; the conversation did not define the
numbers as disjoint, permanently assigned teams. The important design idea is allocating a finite set of selves
across the encounter.
Flow, pressure and solutions
LEVEL-04 — Levels should have a flow. Layout and encounter design should guide the player through meaningful
relationships rather than presenting an unstructured sandbox. This does not require one rigid chronological
solution or forbid spatial branches within a room.
LEVEL-05 — Use time pressure and design clarity. Time pressure comes both from finite tape length and from
external enemies or hazards. Limited lives, paid attempts or a finite retry count were not selected as the
mechanism enforcing deliberate play.
LEVEL-06 — Author a solution, permit alternatives. Each level can be made with a solution in mind, but must

12 / Worked scenarios and expected
consequences
These scenarios preserve concrete examples from the conversation and initial context. They are explanatory tests
of the rules, not a newly approved level list. “Local seconds” refer to the cassette being discussed; a production
implementation maps them onto master beats using the selected tape speed.
Scenario A — Two simultaneous hits
Origin: legacy tutorial candidate. An enemy’s shield recharges immediately after one hit. One tape removes the
shield while another lands the damaging hit at the required overlapping moment. The purpose is to teach why
cooperation can accomplish something a lone sequence of attacks cannot.
The exact simultaneity window, damage handling and whether both hits must be melee are not specified. The rule
must be readable rather than depending on an unexplained single-frame coincidence.
Scenario B — Hold a door for another self
Origin: legacy tutorial candidate retained as relevant context. One self stands on a pressure switch while another
crosses the opened door. Tape speed and end behavior can determine how long that support remains available.
The stop-mode body policy matters here. A tape that ceases playback might keep holding a plate or might stop
contributing entirely; the conversation has not chosen between those behaviors.
Scenario C — Rescue the route after local second six
Origin: current user clarification. Cassette one dies at six, continues recording an interactive hypothetical route,
attacks at ten and flips a switch at twelve. In cassette two’s simulation, those later actions are ineffective if the old
self dies again. Preventing the fatal hit enables them, subject to current targets and collision state.
A door opened only by the original recording ghost must not remain open in the new run without an effective new
activation. This is the clearest test of provisional recording effects versus authoritative playback effects.
Scenario D — A clean take dies at local second twelve
Origin: current user clarification. A previously surviving tape is killed later because another cassette changes
enemy aim. Its remaining three seconds become non-interactive ghost playback even though they were originally
recorded alive.
Expected result: movement continues exactly; damage and switch events no longer count after death; the ghost is
not repeatedly killed; the other tapes continue the attempt.
Scenario E — Redirect the battlefield
Origin: current combat discussion. One self strikes an enemy into a turret’s firing lane or out of the path of
another prerecorded attack. The enemy’s subsequent path is genuinely different.
Expected result: old attacks can miss, new opportunities can appear, and the player can intentionally arrange a
multi-self chain. The engine must not snap the enemy back to preserve a prior outcome.

13 / Equipment, loot and permanent progression
The final progression model
GEAR-01 — A campaign, not a roguelike. Progress accumulates through the descent. There is no run-based reset
of the player’s build after a Slay the Spire-style attempt. The branching-map comparison was exploratory and does
not establish a roguelike structure.
GEAR-02 — Upgrades are physical equipment. The final choice replaces a skill tree and abstract point allocation
with found armor, weapons and related gear. This fits Moxie’s scavenger identity and the motivation to descend
for loot.
GEAR-03 — Change loadouts between levels. “Respeccing” means swapping equipment rather than redistributing
skill points. The user wants this flexibility so an unfortunate configuration does not trap the player with an
impossible puzzle.
GEAR-04 — Keep a generous inventory. The player should not have a super-limited inventory forcing constant
disposal of discovered options. A numeric capacity, weight system, encumbrance mechanic and item-slot count
were not chosen.
GEAR-05 — Gear can enhance or add abilities. Items should strengthen aspects of the existing mechanics and can
introduce new capabilities. The exact categories, progression order and item roster remain to be designed.
Upgrade ideas that were actually discussed
Faster resynchronization: improve the recovery spark’s ability to catch a desynchronized projection. This changes
how quickly an actor regains interaction and is one of the most directly relevant candidates.
More control over knockback and a higher maximum: allow more precise or stronger enemy repositioning. This
can change both combat efficiency and environmental puzzle solutions.
Stronger attacks: reduce the hits needed for tougher ordinary enemies, including the explicit two-hit-to-one-hit
example. The user valued this as a chosen advantage rather than a universal upgrade that merely shifts the next
level’s enemy numbers upward.
Extra time: some form of additional recording allowance was floated, but not fully resolved. It must be reconciled
with the fixed-per-level recording duration and musical alignment rules before use.
On-demand desync: a possible later specialization could eventually allow manual desynchronization. This was a
tentative upgrade idea. The baseline still teaches desync as an emergent environmental property.
Improved dodge or specialized tape hardware: these appeared in assistant examples illustrating equipment, not as
a selected item catalog. They remain possible rather than required.
Choice and puzzle compatibility
The user oscillated between predetermined upgrades and specialization because universal stat increases can feel
less meaningful, while optional powers can make authored puzzles harder to balance. The final resolution keeps
specialization through equipment and allows between-level changes.

14 / Musical design requirements
The soundtrack is generated, not four static stems
MUSIC-01 — Use Harmonograph concepts and capabilities. The user’s Harmonograph is an existing project, not
just an inspirational word or a generic visual instrument. Its mathematical generators, shared harmonic structure
and clock should underpin the cassette-driven soundtrack.
MUSIC-02 — Per-level cassette functions. Each level should probably assign a different mathematical function to
each track. The user affirmed functions in the Harmonograph sense. Musical roles such as bass, drums, melody and
harmony are relevant because the engine supports them, but a permanent one-role-per-cassette assignment was
not finalized.
MUSIC-03 — Coherent combinations at different speeds. Half/normal/double relationships should align the
voices rhythmically. Shared key, scale and chord context should keep them harmonically related. Powers-of-two
speed relationships alone do not guarantee musical beauty; arrangement and harmonic mapping still matter.
MUSIC-04 — Elegant and clean during play. Do not reserve coherence for successful solutions. Earlier incomplete
takes and unusual but valid approaches should still sound as intentional as possible.
Actions contribute to the music
The user compared the desired integration to Beat Saber notes adding to the music and Pistol Whip gunshots
participating in it. These are experience references, not a requirement to copy their scoring or input systems.
MUSIC-05 — Roughly quantize effects to a fine grid. Sound effects should sync to at least something like a
sixteenth-note grid. The user accepted the same general quantization rule for combat and movement sounds. A
sixteenth is the illustrative precision target, not an approved exact tolerance in milliseconds.
MUSIC-06 — Musical irregularity does not invalidate a solution. There is no requirement to play a prescribed tune
or to reject successful actions because they sound “wrong.” The user explicitly rejected steering against
mechanically valid solutions on that basis.
MUSIC-07 — Quantization is not permission to rewrite gameplay. The discussion concerned sounds. It did not
authorize delaying a melee hit, moving a character, changing a switch time or adjusting an enemy result merely to
make the soundtrack neater. Any scheduling offset between the actual event and its audible accent needs a
deliberate latency policy.
A function shaping which notes or accents correspond to events was suggested, but the exact mapping from a
jump, hit, footstep, switch or missile to pitch and timbre remains open. So does the extent to which the musical
layer exists independently of action-triggered sounds.
Death and desync change the composition
MUSIC-08 — Affect the relevant cassette channel. A character’s death or desynchronization should be heard in
that tape’s layer, not represented solely by a visual shader or a global audio effect.
MUSIC-09 — Use generative parameters, not only processing. The user corrected the assumption that this merely
means stacking muffling, tape wobble or phasing effects on a finished stem. Harmonograph exposes mathematical,
pitch, rhythm, gate, velocity and other parameters that can alter the generated composition itself.

15 / Harmonograph: repository and reusable
technical context
Repository identity and source limits
Harmonograph is a repository in Joey Hersh’s GitHub account: j o e y 1 2 7 2 5 / h a r m o n o g r a p h . The supplied page
labels it private. It is distinct from the old jam repository, a s h k a t y / g a m e _ j a m .
This chapter reports the supplied repository-page snapshot. The source code, current repository state and linked
internal documentation were not independently inspected for this consolidation. Relative labels such as “six
minutes ago,” the displayed commit and development status belong to that snapshot, not a fresh verification.
The most relevant integration fact is that the snapshot describes one shared JavaScript engine, with multiple hosts.
The game need not inherit the requirements of the Ableton host merely because that host already exists.
Mathematical and harmonic capabilities
The supplied README describes 66 formulas. Named examples include Fibonacci modulo m, Collatz, Recamán,
Thue–Morse, Nørgård’s infinity series, Stern’s diatomic sequence, primes and bytebeat; logistic, Hénon, Lorenz
and Rössler systems; cellular automata and L-systems; Lissajous figures, rose curves, superformula, spirograph and
times-table circles; rhythm generators and neo-Riemannian harmony.
The page describes scale-degree mapping, gravity toward chord tones, fold modes, leap recovery, contour and
cadence controls. Chromatic notes require an explicit escape. A shared key, scale and chord progression—including
13 scales, Roman-numeral chords and borrowed chords—keeps different voices harmonically related.
Up to eight formula terms or other channels can be combined using amplitudes, ratios, phases and operators.
“Integer Sum” combines raw integer values before modulus. Pitch, gate and velocity sums can be enabled
independently.
Musical transformations include inversion, retrograde, augmentation, diminution and canon, with ABAC phrase
variation. These are especially relevant to cassette speed and backward playback, but they do not by themselves
define the game’s collision, death or reverse-event rules.
Rhythm and instrument roles
The browser supports Melody, Bass, Chord and Drums voices. Gates and velocities can use their own formulas.
Drum voices can sample a contour against a hit threshold, with contour height affecting velocity.
The snapshot lists eleven drum starting points: house, boom bap, trap, drill, Jersey club, dancehall, reggaeton,
jungle, rock, funk/disco and lo-fi. It also mentions Euclidean rhythms, polymeter, modulo gates and a Zeno roll.
These are available references, not a finalized soundtrack genre list for Moxie.
Drum controls include per-voice gates, note maps, velocity curves, timing and Boolean lane logic
(AND/OR/XOR/NOT). Chord controls include extensions from triads to thirteenths, inversions, minimal-motion
voicing, spread and strum. These give state-driven composition more possibilities than post-processing a fixed
audio file.

Determinism and scheduling
The README says the same state produces the same notes and supports seeking, looping and recalling sessions. A
voice is evaluated at step n after speed, phase, retrograde or Sum operations. Values are normalized and mapped
to a scale degree under the chord active at that beat. The compiler emits note events on exact beat boundaries.
This distinction is important for the game: repeating or reversing a contour need not mean restoring an old global
chord. The source describes mapping under the chord active at the current beat. Reusing that principle can
support coherent combinations without globally rewinding harmony.
The browser’s visual playhead follows A u d i o C o n t e x t . g e t O u t p u t T i m e s t a m p ( ) rather than the scheduler, with
optional display delay for Bluetooth. The Web Audio host records events it actually sent, and the heard-pitch
graph uses that record. These are useful synchronization ideas, not proof that the game already has an
audio-accurate replay implementation.
Visualizations available as inspiration
Stack shows multiple continuous formula contours against a shared playhead. Epicycles reconstructs a contour
from its sixteen strongest Fourier harmonics as nested rotating circles. Shape wraps a period around a radial form.
Constellation traces a voice on a pitch-class helix. Other studies are mentioned but not enumerated in the
supplied text.
These visuals can inform runes, temporal machinery and music-responsive architectural patterns. The current
game discussion did not approve copying the full editor UI, exposing mathematical equations to players, or making
every level a literal rendering of a formula.

The browser demo has fifteen voices and built-in Web Audio instruments. Its documented development workflow
uses Node.js 18 or newer, n p m c i , n p m r u n b u i l d : d e m o and n p m r u n d e m o , with a localhost demo server on port

The Ableton Live 12 / Max for Live build has a Conductor and eight MIDI channel devices, plus a local desktop

16 / Interface, readability and cinematic playback
Recording interface
UI-01 — Show the timeline during recording. It is not a post-run-only analysis tool. The selected format is
continuous time with beat markers, rather than only discrete beat ticks or only broad measures.
UI-02 — Match cassette and actor colors. Each timeline should be visibly identified by the color of its cassette in
the UI. These are four versions of Moxie, not a requirement for four separate outfits, genders, bodies or character
designs.
The original jam used red, yellow, blue and green, with keys 1–4. The current conversation selected color
correspondence but did not reconfirm that exact palette or those bindings. The historical colors are available as a
reference, not a final accessibility or art specification.
UI-03 — Communicate actor state. The current recording ghost must be obviously distinct. Playback ghosts, living
desynchronized projections and recovery sparks must not be visually conflated. State-linked musical changes
should reinforce, rather than replace, visual communication.
UI-04 — Show the count-in visually and audibly. “Five, six, seven, eight” is the chosen musical framing. The user
added “as well” after selecting visual presentation, so the intended reading is visual support in addition to an
audible count, not a visual-only replacement.
UI-05 — Expose the selected tape settings. Tape speed and later-unlocked end behavior need an understandable
representation. The precise controls, selector layout and terminology are unchosen. The Walkman is operated
through button prompts and interface first, with supporting character animations appreciated.
Useful working interface implications
A cassette strip can communicate local progress, its mapping onto master beats, alive/ghost/desync state and
commitment status. A current-take retry should be clearly different from a full-attempt reset. These are
straightforward interface consequences of the rules, not a finalized screen design.
The player must be able to follow earlier selves while controlling the new one. Foreground walls, overlapping
effects and bright neon should not conceal important enemies or recovery sparks. The assistant suggested
transparency/cutaway handling for isometric walls; no particular solution was selected.
How to view activity in other rooms is still open. “All prior cassettes are visible during recording” establishes that
they are simulated and shown, rather than hidden by design, but it does not decide whether the camera shows the
whole level, follows the active actor, uses cutaways or supplements off-screen activity with an interface indicator.
A timeline does not automatically imply editable keyframes, free scrubbing, a pause-and-plan editor or arbitrary
beat stepping. None of those features was approved. The player is still performing a take in real time.
The cinematic result
CINE-01 — End-to-end true-speed replay. Once the level is beaten, the player should be able to watch the
combined result against the master beat clock, with all recorded selves happening concurrently.
CINE-02 — Lock gameplay control. The user preferred a proper performance to watch, rather than intervening in
the result. Free camera control was not separately retained. A skip/reset mechanism for failed playback is a
separate requirement from interactive control over a successful encounter.

20 / Development implications and the first

What the first version is for
The user wants a dream-version requirements document and intends to build an MVP to test and play with the
mechanics. That is not a request to cut the entire vision down to the old jam’s deadline or to specify a full
commercial production plan now.
The only clearly deferred feature family is the later challenge/scoring layer: B-side equivalents, optional mastery
challenges, stars and scores. Other systems belong to the discussed vision even where their implementation timing
is undecided. A detailed milestone plan or “must fit in four days” limit has not been set for the new project.
The immediate design uncertainties are best made testable rather than disguised as final balance: the time
mapping, equal-duration recording feel, repeated paths against dynamic geometry, conditional ghost effects,
recovery-spark catch-up, momentum clarity and the music’s response to state.
Required behavior versus implementation choice
The game needs fixed recorded choreography and dynamically resolved outcomes. It needs one coherent temporal
coordinate system, not multiple incompatible calculations that happen to look aligned in one example. It needs a
clear distinction between provisional recording effects and final playback effects. These are behavioral
requirements.
The game does not yet have a selected trajectory storage format, navigation algorithm, physics timestep, replay
serialization scheme, audio integration strategy, rendering pipeline or target platform list. The previous Godot
project is a reference and potential starting point, not evidence that those decisions have already been remade for
the isometric version.
One reasonable implementation interpretation is to preserve local-tape motion and action events, transform them
onto master beats, simulate enemies and world objects from the current run’s state, and gate interaction by the
actor’s life/synchronization state. The recovery position is independent of the fixed visual path. This is a
conceptual decomposition, not mandatory class names or a new engine architecture.
Replay integrity
A successful cinematic must reproduce the actual resolved outcome. It should not trigger a new boss learning step,
choose new random decisions, or evaluate ghost-only provisional kills as genuine. The document requires outcome
fidelity but leaves open whether the implementation records scene states, records deterministic simulation inputs
and seeds, or uses another method.
The old ring-buffer approach can inform recording storage, but must not be accepted merely because it existed.
Likewise, the supplied Harmonograph determinism claim concerns note generation from musical state, not the
determinism of the game’s physics or enemy system.
Tests that follow directly from the design
The acceptance scenarios in the next chapter turn the existing discussion into checks. They are not new
mechanics. An implementation can pass them in different architectures provided it preserves the experience.

This register stops at details already raised or directly exposed by the discussed mechanics. It is not a new scope

Time and tape boundaries
OPEN-01 — Exact speed menu. Half/middle/double is established as the relationship. Is 4× retained from the old
list, and what labels does the interface use?
OPEN-02 — Overall encounter horizon. Does the slowest selected tape, slowest allowed option or an authored
master-bar duration define it? This changes fast-only attempts and the availability of repeated traversals.
OPEN-03 — Local bars versus master bars. The equal real-time take, inverse playback duration and
speed-sensitive count-in need one unambiguous clock definition. The illustrative equations are not a substitute for
agreeing that terminology.
OPEN-04 — Fast/slow tradeoffs. Earlier desired harder fast play and shorter reach do not follow automatically
from the later equal-local-time movement model. Preserve the intent as a test target rather than hard-coding
contradictory rules.
OPEN-05 — End behavior details. What remains physical after stop? Does a dead cassette reset at a repeat
boundary? What resources or spawned objects persist? What happens to recovery state on teleport or reversal?
OPEN-06 — Reverse event semantics. Specify switch behavior under multiple actors, continuous interactions,
projectile return behavior and homing missiles. Global history reversal is already ruled out; the object-level
inverses are not all defined.
State, collision and outcome
OPEN-07 — Authoritative result after the final active ghost. Decide how to exclude provisional post-death
interactions from the final win result while still letting the player finish the take interactively.
OPEN-08 — Survivor evaluation. At what beat is survival checked? Does an alive but desynchronized actor
qualify? What about a stopped tape? Does a level require someone physically at an exit as well as alive?
OPEN-09 — Recovery tuning. Choose spark speed, acceleration if any, catch-up tolerance, route selection and
replanning behavior. Define what happens when no route exists or catch-up never occurs.
OPEN-10 — Collision permissions. Decide actor-to-actor blocking, friendly fire, projectile interception, movable
objects, enemy bodies obstructing a projection, environmental hazards and recovery-spark interaction layers.
OPEN-11 — Active-player desync. The established examples concern earlier recordings obstructed by later
actions. Whether live control itself can desynchronize before a special gear ability, and how that would record, was
not defined.

24 / Legacy concept inventory and original build
context
This chapter preserves the substance of the initial documents without treating every brainstorm as part of the
current game. The full archive, including its original wording, links and message excerpts, follows in Appendix A.
Historical assertions below are attributed to that archive, not independently verified claims about the referenced

Other games considered before the cassette concept
The initial catalog contained a racing deckbuilder in the Balatro/Slay the Spire family, with strong inspiration from
Heat; a D&D-inspired turn-based game; XCOM combat combined with B/STS cards; and a fashion game in which
designing clothes for a runway was also preparing for combat on a battlefield.
The flywheel-racing variant emphasized building acceleration across laps without losing traction. Braking could
break a multiplier/streak, compared with Beat Saber. Upgrading the car through cards, rhythm checks or currency
was considered, with cards favored for tension and chance. These are separate concepts and ancestry for
momentum/multiplier thinking, not requirements for Moxie’s equipment or combat systems.
An expanding-world puzzle used a tiny isometric world that grows every time the goal is reached. Lego-like blocks
would be added, new obstacles could include a simulated waterfall or enemies, and the player might choose from
three world additions. Another fragment proposed 4D exploration in a 2D game, possibly by looping music tracks.
Other variants included falling repeatedly from the top to the bottom of a looping platformer; a grappling hook
used to circle platforms and build speed, with a shift action creating a bouncing shield; Recur-inspired
forward/backward time controlled by moving right/left; and a conveyor-belt level fragment. The reference to
rebuilding The Witcher 3 in four days was a joke, not a scope target.
The cassette idea’s ancestry
The time-loop FPS idea originated in the redwoods discussion. It was retooled as a platformer/boss rush: four
tracks, failure if the boss is not killed by the fourth recording, previous performances playing simultaneously, and a
boss able to change actions because it is outside the loop.
The brainstorm included speed choices of 0.5×/1×/2×/4×, momentum attacks, a late powerup to overwrite one
thread, and light cooperative-style puzzles compared with The Legend of Zelda: Tri Force Heroes. My Friend
Pedro was named for combat; another idea had two tracks fighting while two enabled routes through the
environment.
The winning jam direction was a small 2D brawler/platformer with four colored cassettes, combo-heavy tag-team
combat, possible waves and a boss, and tape length as a resource. TowerFall, Smash Bros, fighting games and Your
Only Move Is Hustle were feel references. A different weapon per cassette was described as beyond comfortable
jam scope, not an implemented feature.
The current version inherits four-self coordination, tape identity, time-scaled force and the possibility of an
outside-loop boss. It changes the perspective, puzzle priority, protagonist/world, progression, music generation
and detailed state rules.

Old time and interaction rules
The old discussion deliberately avoided rewind to sidestep questions about undoing enemy death. It selected play,
loop and write, with enemies remaining dead within the run. The new reverse-action mechanic is a later revision
and should not be deleted because the archive says “no rewinding.”
A useful original causality example survives: if tape one fires at an enemy at time five, tape two may move the
enemy before the projectile arrives, so the old shot no longer kills it. That idea directly anticipates the current
live-enemy/fixed-performance model.
The old death rule advanced the player to the next tape and left a ghost with collisions but no damage or
interactions until saved by a later self. The new design instead continues recording a constrained, interactive
hypothetical ghost, then recalculates effectiveness during subsequent playback. These systems are related but not
interchangeable.
The archive’s high-five when two tracks pass in opposite directions is a personality flourish, not a current required
mechanic. Track-specific enemies, track-colored zones, hue-rotated clothing, screen tints, grabbing/grappling and
a combo charge meter also remain legacy ideas unless reselected.
Special pickups included time freeze and double speed. Mixing controls could strengthen tapes;
drum/chord/melody roles could grant different powers; genres could determine abilities. None of those gameplay
mappings is confirmed in the current Harmonograph requirements.
Loop-themed level ideas
The source listed an airport baggage carousel, factory conveyor belt, race track with cars potentially moving
forward/backward in time, circuit board, vinyl record player, lazy river, hiking trail and bowling alley. The
record-player idea included hitting the needle to stop or start the soundtrack.
These motifs may inspire mechanical geometry or themes, but the current campaign setting is an ancient,
retrofitted dungeon descent. They are not eight mandatory modern-world biomes. Laser gates and
button-controlled passages from the old build are more directly reusable interaction concepts.
Earlier story and character
The jam story moved through a musician reclaiming space from AI-generated music, a loved-one or pet rescue
template, then evil music police stopping the heroine’s music. Robo Bee police drones and an ettercap appeared in
the build. A joke ending had a small dog follow the heroine and urinate on a music cop.
The old protagonist moved from cyberpunk/android/magical-girl concepts to a rebellious cyborg. A mask reduced
mouth-animation work; facial expression would use eyebrows. Specific ideas included a robotic arm and knee, a
piano-key jacket accent, low-cut techwear pants, an angular top/collar and recognizable shoes.
Weapon sketches included axe-guitar or axe-bass, keyboard-axe, harp, lyre/sickle, flute sword, electric banjo-axe,
sitar-axe and balalaika. The axe-guitar/bass direction won under time pressure. The present game confirms melee
and equipment weapons, but not that the final weapon must be an axe-guitar.
The new Moxie concept replaces the old protagonist’s synthetic identity and mask requirement. Some visual
ancestry—streetwear, music equipment, low-rise pants and bold color—remains compatible. The new female-gaze,
expressive human scavenger brief takes precedence.

[C05] Reactive enemies and fixed attacks. Closest-target firing and farthest-target pursuit were examples. Past

[C06] Ghost correction. “The effects should impact that run, but no later runs.” Active post-death recording

[C07] Physical resynchronization. The user rejected merely checking whether the projection is inside a wall,
proposed a fast but finite naive catch-up agent, and wanted alternate routes and opening doors to matter. Desync
should be emergent; the spark should be visible but small and ephemeral. The wall/turret example is

[C08] Coordination and boss identity. Most fights require coordination at the encounter scale, though one self can
handle a weaker enemy or two. Some threats need two, three or four selves. At least one boss should exist outside
the loop and respond across recordings.
[C09] Combat, missiles and survival. Light/heavy melee and a dodge were retained. The user specified tape speed
and actual movement feeding momentum, with momentum determining knockback, stagger and damage. Missiles
target nearest once at launch. Player cassettes die in one hit, ordinary enemies mostly in one with some two/three,
bosses have health bars, and “Any one must survive.”
[C10] Retry and interface rules. On editing an old cassette: “Wipes all of them.” Current takes can be retried
before advancing. The timeline is visible while recording and uses continuous time with beat markers. A
visual/audible five-six-seven-eight count-in responds to tape speed. Playback can start automatically.
[C11] Level and challenge structure. A level is one round of cassette recording, not separate cassette-reset
encounters. Typical combinations include 1+3, 2+2 or one four-self challenge. One-self teaching levels are
allowed. Use time pressure from both tapes and hazards, clear flow, intended solutions with alternate approaches,
full failed playback with early reset, and later—not immediate MVP—B-sides/stars/scores.
[C12] Progression’s final form. The user rejected roguelike reset, explored a branching map and skill tree, then
settled on a fixed campaign path with revisits and found equipment. Armor/weapons can be swapped between

[C13] Isometric selection. Jumping/verticality were recognized as incidental to the original mental picture: “Let’s
go isometric.” Interconnected rooms and spatial navigation are welcome without preserving precision-platforming
as the main activity.
[C14] Music and final performance. Keep all four layers as elegant/clean/cohesive as possible. Actions’ sounds
should roughly quantize to something like sixteenth notes using the same rule for combat/movement.
Mechanically valid but odd rhythms remain valid. Extra music-linked visual reward belongs in the locked, cinematic,
true-timeline playback.
[C15] Parametric state music and enemy limits. The user corrected a post-effects-only description: Harmonograph

by and large do not change actual wave functions. Musical visualization in the world was welcomed.

REVISION 1 / DESIGN DECISIONS
Applies to all preceding chapters; later user requirements supersede earlier wording.
Revision 1  |  Controlling addendum  |  16 November 2026
1
This addendum records three explicit refinements made after the original illustrated document: ranged attacks form a
multi-weapon family; melee weapons are interchangeable loot with distinct properties; and four visible cassette decks
with tape-speed readouts are mandatory, first-class gameplay interface elements.
New controlling requirements
WEAPON-01  /  Multiple viable ranged weapons
Guided missiles are ONE ranged weapon option, not the entire ranged combat system. Players can find and equip other
ranged weapons with different projectile behavior, speeds, range, penetration, and trajectories. Melee remains the primary
combat emphasis.
WEAPON-02  /  Homing missile downside
A missile is intentionally VERY SLOW relative to faster direct-fire projectiles. It acquires the nearest enemy exactly once on
launch, then tracks that chosen target. Its long travel time is a tactical drawback and gives the changing battlefield time to
affect the outcome.
WEAPON-03  /  Other projectile archetypes
Examples to explore include fast shots that pierce multiple enemies, projectiles that penetrate walls, and weapons using
arcing or vertical trajectories. These are design directions, not a settled complete item roster or weapon balance sheet.
WEAPON-04  /  Varied melee equipment
Different found melee weapons must be viable, with meaningful differences such as reach, attack arc, windup, recovery,
attack speed, impact transfer, stagger and knockback efficiency. Weapon selection should alter viable strategies, not simply
reskin one sword.
UI-06  /  Four persistent cassette UI elements
All four cassette identities must exist as unmistakable, first-class on-screen UI components, not merely small optional
indicators or a single generic timeline. Their colors must match the corresponding Moxie copies in the world.
UI-07  /  Explicit tape-speed visualization
Each cassette displays its own selected tape speed as a prominent visual value and readout. The UI must make half/double
relationships intuitive and visibly connect playback rate to tape duration, beat alignment and active motion.
The basic facts are settled; exact weapon models, ammunition systems, equipment slot layout, cassette deck placement and
visual animation are still design tasks. Do not treat the examples in this addendum as confirmed statistics or final art.

The rule: fixed trigger, simulated projectile
A past cassette always performs a firing action at the same recorded master-beat point and recorded position. The launched
object may use its own world simulation: collision, travel time, trajectory and target selection are determined by the current
encounter. A projectile can hit, miss, pierce or be obstructed differently on later runs without rewriting the performer.

Melee weapon family
Weapons found in the dungeon should have different timing, contact shapes and momentum transfer. Example design axes:

level attempts, avoiding changes that silently reinterpret an already committed tape. Exact projectile reverse-playback behavior
remains unresolved.

What must be genuinely visible in production
Cassette presence and status
All four cassette objects have distinct color channels and readable states: unavailable/not yet recorded, active recording,
committed playback, stopped or reverse/loop mode when unlocked. The active cassette must be unmistakable.
Speed is a first-class control and readout

changing reel/transport behavior and a clear rate label should make the time relationship apparent.
Timeline and alignment
Show a continuous-time timeline with beat markers WHILE recording, plus explicit mapping to master beats and tape
progress. Prior recordings appear as simultaneous performers; future unrecorded selves must not appear.
Feedback and legibility
Show death/ghost/desync indicators distinctly, as well as fixed take commitment, retry current take and reset entire attempt.
Supporting cassette artwork and Walkman animations can reinforce these controls but cannot replace them.

REVISION 2 / EQUIPMENT EFFECTS
Supersedes any earlier implication that armor or clothing is cosmetic only.
Controlling addendum | 16 November 2026
1
APPAREL-01 / All four clothing slots must have gameplay effects
Helmets, shirts or tops, pants, and shoes are distinct equippable categories. Each slot can affect one or more
aspects of gameplay, and the equipment system should expose those effects clearly. Clothing is not restricted
to appearance or a flat defense statistic.
HELMETS / HEADGEAR
Possible effects: enemy targeting or awareness, protection against a specific hazard, beat or timeline
readability, or a cassette-related perk. A visible helmet need not obscure Moxie's signature yellow headphones;
appearance compatibility is an art and UX requirement.
SHIRTS / TOPS / JACKETS
Possible effects: attack power or type, damage/impact properties, interaction radius, desynchronization
behavior, or other utility. Her signature crop-top and layered streetwear silhouette should remain a
recognizable visual baseline.
PANTS / LEGWEAR
Possible effects: mobility or acceleration, directional control, momentum retention, dodge recovery, or
carrying/utility properties. Examples are candidate design axes, not finalized item statistics.
SHOES / BOOTS
Possible effects: traction, movement speed, dodge characteristics, terrain handling, collision behavior, or
momentum transfer. Footwear can change how a level is solved rather than merely improve a score.

REVISION 2 / EQUIPMENT EFFECTS
Equipment architecture, loadout swapping, and acceptance requirements.
Controlling addendum | 16 November 2026
2
APPAREL-02 / Loadouts, tradeoffs, and puzzle viability
• Moxie scavenges equipment during her descent. Weapons and wearable gear may be found as loot, stored in
a generous inventory, and swapped BETWEEN levels. This is the primary customization/respec mechanism;
no abstract skill-tree points are required.
• Every named apparel slot may have an active effect, passive modifier, or conditional ability. Equipment can
grant new abilities as well as adjust existing ones. Different pieces should support meaningful playstyles
without every upgrade being a strictly superior replacement.
• The slots coexist with multiple viable melee weapons and multiple ranged weapon options. Slow one-lock
homing missiles, fast piercing projectiles, wall-penetrating shots, and arcing/vertical trajectories are potential
ranged archetypes, not universal attack properties.
• Mandatory levels must remain completable with equipment available at that point, and the player may revisit
older levels and change loadouts to experiment with alternate solutions. Specific gear lists, numerical stats,
inventory capacity, and exact slot restrictions remain to be playtested or designed.
APPAREL-03 / Visual and interface clarity
• The loadout interface must plainly identify helmet/headgear, shirt/top, pants, shoes, melee weapon, and
ranged weapon slots and state their mechanical effects in player-readable terms.
• Equipped clothing should be visibly reflected on the character when practical, while preserving the
recognizable Moxie identity: yellow headphones, belt Walkman, scrappy pigtails, streetwear crop top and
low-rise silhouette as the base design.
• The four cassette objects and their individual tape-speed indicators remain mandatory, first-class gameplay
HUD elements, alongside the live beat-marked timeline. Apparel changes must not hide or replace these core
controls.
Acceptance criteria
APP-TEST-01: Equip and swap a helmet, shirt, pants, and shoes; each category can demonstrably change a
gameplay outcome.
APP-TEST-02: Changing a clothing slot updates its displayed effect and preserves the distinction between
equipment categories.
APP-TEST-03: A valid compulsory level can be completed using at least the intended baseline loadout
available before entering it.
